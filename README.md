# Aero Formula · 翼构方程 · 翼の方程式

A static, browser-based 3D aircraft design and flight challenge game. Built with TypeScript, Svelte 5, Three.js and Vite, without a general-purpose physics engine. Supports English, Simplified Chinese and Japanese, desktop browsers, and portrait or landscape touch screens. The interface defaults to your browser language. Use the language selector to change it; manual selection is saved in the current browser.

## Run locally

```sh
npm install
npm run dev
```

Open the local URL printed in the terminal. For a production build:

```sh
npm run check
npm test
npm run build
npx vite preview --host 0.0.0.0
```

Deploy `dist/` to any static host. If the Chinese web font cannot load, the interface uses the local sans-serif font; play remains available.

## Challenges and aircraft

- Each home-screen challenge button opens its aircraft workshop. Compatible challenge switches preserve the current aircraft, saved selection and undo history. Entering an incompatible competition loads that competition's default aircraft and resets history; leaving a laboratory for competition also resets history so undo cannot restore an incompatible type.
- **Birdman:** entering with an ineligible aircraft restores the default unpowered Birdman design; an eligible design stays unchanged. Launch from the lakeside platform and compete for horizontal distance.
- **Mountain descent:** launch from an elevated mountaintop catapult, accelerating to 20 m/s over 20 m. Timing starts at the starting gate. Follow 30 checkpoints through a 5400 m course with winding valleys, a steep descent and plain ruins. Mountain and dive gates retain flat tops at their center reference height plus 125 m. Plain-ruin gates span the canyon and follow the terrain plus 62.5 m. Restrictions exist only on each unpassed gate plane, not between gates. Red-striped masks fade in within 90 m as you approach; passing a gate immediately removes its frame and masks, including when flying back. Touching a blocked part of an active gate plane ends the run. Terrain and ruins remain physical obstacles. Completed runs rank by time. Reset design explicitly loads the high-performance glider.
- **Low-level flight:** start on a rail 2 m above a 400 m runway. The first 300 m stays level; the last 100 m curves smoothly upward to a 15° exit. Actual engine thrust drives the roll, with gravity and rolling resistance along the rail. Early release uses the upward free acceleration of the whole aircraft. Rail-end release preserves tangent velocity and attitude. The exit has 80 m of terrain clearance, followed by an 80 m transition into the valley. Timing and scoring start at the starting gate. Follow 30 checkpoints along a 4800 m river course. Flying lower and reaching the finish sooner increases the score. Above 25 m of clearance, the HUD warns and deducts points continuously, increasing the rate with uninterrupted time above the limit. Returning within 25 m resets that timer. Only finished runs enter the records. Reset design explicitly loads the basic single-engine aircraft with 100% throttle, adjustable before launch.
- Birdman and conventional aircraft are separate competition types. Birdman accepts only Birdman designs; mountain descent and low-level flight accept only conventional designs. Test space and wind tunnel accept both and expose an aircraft-type selector. Switching to Birdman removes engines, fuel capacity, ailerons and the horizontal-tail elevator while retaining geometry; switching to conventional enables those control surfaces. Type changes support undo and redo. Imports keep their original type and remain saved but unloaded when incompatible.
- `W/S` command nose-down/nose-up, `A/D` command left/right roll, and `Q/E` command left/right yaw. Birdman's first two axes move the pilot's center of mass; conventional aircraft use the elevator and ailerons. Touch controls provide all three axes. Control surfaces move gradually, including their return to neutral.
- The bottom-left circular stick shows actual pitch and roll input, with elevator and aileron trim beside it. `J/K` and `H/L` change trim by 1 percentage point. `T` toggles automatic elevator trim for conventional aircraft, combining pitch-rate damping with slow trim integration. Integration stops when it would deepen structural or actuator saturation; reverse correction remains available. `Y` toggles structure-adaptive control authority: current-direction full-stick loads are predicted with the actual aerodynamic and beam models, targeting at most 90% of the attached structure's failure limit. If neutral-control loads already exceed that target, commands go to zero without suppressing real overload or fracture.
- Each challenge keeps separate records. Records from different physics versions do not share a leaderboard. Replays retain the challenge, design, control inputs and launch disturbance. The former standard, assisted and experimental modes have been removed.
- Replay physics and 3D geometry both use an independent snapshot of the recorded aircraft. The timeline supports mouse, touch and keyboard seeking in both directions, including from results. Recordings save complete deterministic keyframes at fixed one-second intervals, including controls, fuel, deformation and fractures. Seeking restores the nearest earlier keyframe without integration, then advances only the remaining fraction. A bounded full-state cache covers nearby frames before and after the current position for fine scrubbing. Wing-load waveforms use saved per-frame substep maxima, grouped into 0.1 s maxima without replay-time analysis. The read-only stick and throttle show recorded inputs. Obsolete seeks are cancelled; a high-contrast loading overlay indicates outstanding work without blocking further input. Seeking never changes personal bests.
- Flight camera selection, including changes made with `C`, is remembered for retries, replays and page reloads. When browser storage is unavailable, it remains available for the current session.
- Mountain-descent and low-level-flight results overlay a G-load curve and replace minimum speed with the maximum G across all accepted physics steps. G is the magnitude of non-gravitational acceleration at the aircraft CG, including launch and support loads: supported rest is 1 G and free fall is 0 G. Birdman results do not display G metrics.
- Workshop tabs are Fuselage, Main wing, Horizontal tail, Vertical tail and Power. Mount positions and installation angles live on their respective component tabs; fuselage endpoints and cockpit position live on Fuselage. Birdman hides Power. All components share a deep-blue wind-tunnel-style room with grids and scales. Hover highlights a component; clicking selects it and synchronizes the sidebar. Sidebar selection also selects the component.
- The upper-left HUD is challenge-specific. Mountain descent shows airspeed and signed vertical velocity: a thick red up arrow for climb ≥0.1 m/s, a green down arrow for descent ≤−0.1 m/s, and a thick gray horizontal mark between those thresholds. Low-level flight shows airspeed, ground clearance and remaining fuel. Birdman retains distance, altitude, time, angle of attack, lift-to-drag ratio and energy use. Other challenges do not show Birdman's energy instruments.
- Results use actual sampled data. Mountain descent shows airspeed and distance–altitude charts. Low-level flight adds a dashed scoring ceiling along the actual track: local terrain or river height plus 25 m. The airspeed chart uses simulation time since starting the flight, including the launch or runway roll. Scored distance accumulates only after the starting gate; earlier samples have zero distance. Pauses produce no samples, and replays resample the same flight. Birdman retains altitude, mechanical-energy and energy-use curves.
- The results panel fades in on the right over 650 ms; portrait phones use a scrollable bottom panel. The background is slightly transparent while text and charts remain opaque. The camera slowly orbits from the final flight view without jumping to a preset angle. Vertical dragging changes elevation; horizontal motion remains automatic, including while the mouse is held. Reduced-motion preferences stop automatic orbiting.
- Wing and fuselage grazing contacts with terrain, ruins or launchers use nonelastic frictionless impulses and can continue into flight. Wing reactions act at the actual contact samples: bending and torsion pass through the contacted strip and its inboard joints, alongside the same rigid-body inertial response, causing elastic deformation or fracture according to beam strength. Both initial impacts and accepted midpoint support reactions contribute. Elastic shape changes are constrained against solids while preserving joint continuity and the full stress used for fracture decisions. The quasi-static beam model averages impact impulses over the nominal physics interval (at most 1/60 s), rather than the tiny intervals used to locate first contact; it does not resolve transient elastic vibration. Nose or cockpit contact ends the flight; a fuselage resting on non-launcher ground ends it below 1.5 m/s. Stationary wing contact alone does not trigger this rule. These structural collision changes advance the physics version to `adaptive-damped-v10`; older physics recordings are rejected explicitly.
- Light lake or river skimming can continue into flight. Submerged parts experience simplified water drag and buoyancy, and the aircraft remains controllable. Water loads can slow, turn or break the actual beam structure. Nose immersion ends the run, as does body contact with water at a total speed below 1.5 m/s. Low speed in air does not trigger this rule. Riverbeds and banks remain solid terrain. Splashes begin at the actual contact point rather than waiting for results.

## Keyboard, gamepad and flight-stick input

Open **Input settings** in the home-screen header (also available during flight). Keyboard, standard gamepad and flight-stick profiles have separate mappings. Keyboard and button actions support multiple bindings; each full-axis control uses one physical axis. Each action has clearing and **Restore default**; reset the current profile or all input settings independently. Changes remain drafts until you select **Save** at the top or bottom. Clicking outside settings or pressing Escape opens **Save / Don't save / Cancel**. Discard restores mappings, calibration and device preferences from before opening; Cancel keeps editing. A failed save leaves the draft open and reports the storage error.

- **Standard gamepad:** left stick controls roll/pitch; right stick looks around. Left/right shoulders decrease/increase throttle at 35% per second. Analog triggers control left/right yaw by their difference: both fully pressed give neutral, left 100% and right 50% give 50% left yaw. Bottom face button starts/pauses, top restarts, left cycles cameras. Other actions start unbound.
- **Flight stick:** axes X/Y control roll/pitch, Z controls yaw, and the base slider controls absolute throttle (default axis 3, reversed). The eight-way hat controls look direction; diagonals combine both directions. Buttons 0/1 start/pause and restart. Other actions start unbound. Axis/button numbers vary by device: use capture or the manual binding editor to match your hardware.
- Controller pitch, roll and yaw use single signed-axis rows rather than separate positive/negative mappings. View axes use the same full-axis model. Every analog binding has its own **Invert input** switch, including in the binding editor. Assigning a new full axis replaces that control's previous axis or trigger pair.
- The latest assignment owns each physical key, button or axis within its profile. Conflicting bindings are removed from other controls, with a message naming each affected control. Distinct hat directions remain independently assignable. Changing inversion does not remove its own binding.
- Bound analog inputs show their live calibrated output; unbound meters are gray. Button chips change background while physically held and return on release, including after near-zero analog trigger values. Feedback follows the current binding and device, including rebinding while another button is held or disconnecting a controller.
- The bottom of settings contains the same circular stick and throttle display used in flight replay, plus a centered yaw meter. These displays preview the selected draft profile without affecting flight. Throttle-increase/decrease buttons adjust only the preview at 35% per second.
- Devices connect/disconnect without reloading. Press a controller button if the browser has not exposed it yet. Standard-mapped devices use the gamepad profile; other devices use the flight-stick profile. Override the device profile and choose a controller explicitly when needed. Device profile overrides and calibration persist by device ID.
- Calibration exposes live raw axis values, minimum/center/maximum, deadzone and inversion. Move through the full travel to inspect the endpoints, enter the measured limits, then release centered axes and capture their center. Throttle uses the calibrated endpoint range rather than the centered-axis deadzone.
- Held keyboard inputs take priority over the corresponding controller axis; releasing them returns control to the controller. Touch/mouse stick and yaw controls take priority while engaged. A throttle slider must move meaningfully before taking control; keyboard throttle or the on-screen throttle slider releases that ownership until the hardware slider moves again.
- Start/pause, restart and camera changes fire once per press. Opening settings suspends flight and gameplay input; closing restores the previous flight state without firing held buttons. Blur, visibility changes, disconnect and retries clear transient inputs. Replays keep their recorded flight controls, while camera inspection remains available.
- Keyboard trim keeps operating-system key repeat, including remapped keys. Holding a trim key continues stepping by 1 percentage point; holding start/pause or a toggle does not repeatedly activate it.

The independent `src/input.ts` module owns polling, bindings, calibration, priority, draft transactions and persistence; `src/InputSettings.svelte` owns the compact responsive settings interface. `src/InputReadout.svelte` shares stick, throttle and yaw displays with flight replay. Existing directional controller settings migrate to full-axis mappings in memory and are written only on explicit Save. Gamepad API requires a compatible browser and an allowed secure context (HTTPS or localhost). Real hardware layouts still require device-specific calibration.

## Materials, deformation and wing failure

Choose Sitka spruce, 6061-T6 aluminum or finished carbon/epoxy laminate separately for the main wing, horizontal tail and vertical tail. Each surface has an independent material amount from 50% to 200%, defaulting to 100%. Material amount changes beam mass, bending and torsional stiffness, and section load capacity. Pilot, fuselage and fixed-density skin mass do not scale with it. Beams use preset sections; chordwise width, normal height and wall thickness are not exposed as controls. Density, elastic moduli and strengths come from the shared material catalog.

The four system aircraft use the supplied Birdman basic, high-performance glider, basic twin-engine and basic single-engine definitions. Materials and challenge configuration are independent: changing challenges retains current materials and amounts. Old designs without amounts load at 100%. Saving, importing, exporting and undo preserve each surface's amount. More mass does not guarantee higher speed: it reduces deceleration from a given drag force but increases wing loading and takeoff requirements.

Surfaces bend and break at the existing 0.8 m aerodynamic boundaries. Rendered sections are spaced at no more than 0.2 m and interpolate continuously. Every overloaded, connected boundary can break in the same physics step, processed from the tip inward so an inboard break cannot conceal an outboard failure. Each separated section becomes an independent rigid body, retaining its position, orientation and velocity field. It continues to receive aerodynamic forces from its own local airflow and can break again. A detached wing does not itself end the run; a fragment landing does not end the main aircraft's run. Body contact, course boundaries and finish conditions still determine results. The attitude and structure panel shows ultimate-limit utilization separately for the main wing, horizontal tail and vertical tail, alongside deformation and fracture count. Utilization comes from the equivalent-beam model, not a certified flight envelope.

Returning to Design restores complete, neutral surfaces. Starting another flight removes fragments and establishes static sag under supported self-weight. The runway roll and catapult continuously evaluate real aerodynamic loads, constraint inertia and deformation; leaving the rail does not reset beam nodes. Launch height uses the full deformed collision shape to maintain clearance. Fragments share the wing's vertex buffers. Cleanup releases only fragment-owned indices and draw state, preserving GPU resources still used by the wing.

The structure is a quasi-static equivalent beam. It does not model full soft-body behavior, manufacturability, flutter, elastic vibration, plasticity, fatigue or wakes between fragments. The interface labels engineering-equivalent directional material values. Old designs receive default materials and sections; unknown materials are rejected on import. New records remain separate from earlier physics versions. See [formulas, parameters and sources](docs/physics.md#材料与分段弹性结构).

## Jet power and fuel

The Power tab offers one jet engine model, with unpowered, single-engine or twin-engine layouts. A single engine stays on the fuselage centerline; twins stay on either side. Lateral offset is fixed. Adjust fore-aft position and signed vertical offset from −0.5 to 1 m: negative is below, positive above. A single engine's valid height ranges can be discontinuous, automatically skipping overlap with the fuselage, cockpit, canopy and nose cone; the interface lists the current valid ranges. Mounts farther from the fuselage receive automatic supports. Engines and supports contribute to mass, center of mass, inertia and thrust moment. The isolated Power view hides the environment.

The fuel tank stays inside the cockpit shell. Adjust its longitudinal position and capacity, up to 40 L; shortening the cockpit rear section lowers this limit. Position bounds depend on capacity and cockpit space so the entire tank remains inside. Removing engines also removes the tank, including for imported and loaded designs. Flights start with a full tank at 0.8 kg/L. Design instruments and 3D endpoint markers show the actual full-to-empty center-of-mass range. Empty tanks retain their shell mass. Fuel use changes mass, center of mass and inertia; thrust stops when fuel runs out. Birdman automatically removes power and fuel.

Throttle starts at 100%. Hold `1` to increase it or `2` to decrease it at 35% per second, including before starting the flight. Trim and flight-assist switches also work before launch. Automatic pitch trim changes the trim bar color and locks manual pitch-trim input until disabled. The throttle slider's solid fill shows actual RPM; its contrasting thumb marks target RPM. Engine acceleration uses a 1.8 s time constant and deceleration 0.9 s. Actual thrust and fuel use follow RPM, not the target. Replays record throttle inputs. Engine data lives in `src/engines.ts` as `engineCatalog`.

Each engine produces 360 N of static thrust at sea level, or 720 N for twins. Air density reduces thrust and fuel flow with altitude; forward inlet airspeed reduces net thrust through ram drag. Exhaust has three blue-flame layers, a bright core and four flame segments. Intensity follows actual thrust and pulses with simulation time. Pausing freezes it; zero thrust extinguishes it.

## Birdman walkthrough

1. Select Birdman on the home screen. Edit the fuselage endpoints and cockpit position. The pilot is fixed at 60 kg. Legal legacy pilot masses also normalize to 60 kg, so old files provide no weight advantage.
2. On the Main wing, Horizontal tail or Vertical tail tab, drag four control points directly on the 3D aircraft. Drag root chord 1–4 or tip chord 2–3 to move both endpoints together. Screen-vertical motion has light snapping; hold `Ctrl` to disable it. Numeric inputs do not snap. Left and right wings remain symmetric.
3. Use Design and Flight at the top of the workshop. Design starts in free perspective; the upper-left buttons select orthographic views. Rotating an orthographic view returns to free perspective. Switching components retains the camera, and no design view automatically recenters. Dragging the nose or tail changes that end without moving the other. The cockpit and wing mounts remain supported by the fuselage. Main-wing dihedral accepts −25° to 25° and rotates the actual wing without stretching its shape, length or mass.
   Workshop lengths use 0.01 m steps and angles 0.1°. Tail vertices edit independently. The vertical tail can extend down to −0.5 m. Drag the horizontal-tail mount toward the fin tip to attach it; its root chord then follows the fin-tip endpoints. Fuselage endpoints cannot cross the origin (nose ≥0.1 m, tail ≤−0.1 m) or leave mounted endpoints unsupported. Conventional cockpits slide wherever the full shell and canopy fit. Their front and canopy stay fixed relative to the cockpit; rear length changes shell mass, inertia and maximum fuel capacity. Cockpit drag acts at its actual geometry centers.
   The Horizontal tail tab provides a “Mount horizontal tail on fin tip” switch. Drag snapping uses a 0.30–0.36 m radius; hold `Ctrl` to bypass it. When attached, the mount diamond stays exactly at the fin tip rather than moving outward with crowded handles. Turn the switch off to return the tail to the fuselage. Both operations support undo and redo.
4. Enter Flight, then select the large Start flight button or press Space. Birdman's published rules supply the 10 m sloped runway length, 3.5° downward slope and 10 m front-edge height above water. The 5 m/s² acceleration and 10 m/s speed after two seconds are game launch settings, not official speed rules. Land joins the rear of the platform smoothly. A recessed central steam channel contains metal rails. A telescoping tow arm connects the launch carriage to the fuselage; the carriage accelerates with the aircraft, releases at the edge and remains at the rail end. Starting again resets it. Steam follows physics time and stops while paused. A random ±1° roll disturbance builds smoothly throughout acceleration, with no attitude jump at release. Replays restore the same disturbance. Solid signs below the runway sides display length and slope without facing the camera; distance labels lie flat over the water.
   Birdman and mountain-descent catapults eject dense steam to both sides along the launch rail. Steam expands and fades after release; simulation time controls emission and motion.
5. Use `W/S` or Up/Down to move the pilot fore-aft; `A/D` or Left/Right to move laterally; `Q/E` to use the rudder. On phones, hold a control button and release it to return to neutral. Space starts, pauses or resumes. `C` cycles through chase, offset chase, side, pilot and free cameras. Inputs, selects and editable content retain native keyboard behavior.
6. Platform contact changes linear and angular velocity through rigid-body collision without ending the run. Keep flying, then inspect distance, time, speed, altitude and the distance–altitude chart after water termination. Results overlay remaining mechanical energy and instantaneous energy use on the same distance axis. Altitude is a solid cyan line on the left axis in m; mechanical energy is a dashed orange line on the right axis at 0–100%; energy use is a dotted dark-cyan line on the outer-right axis in J/m. Energy use is dissipated power divided by horizontal speed; undefined values leave gaps. Results omit the former energy-allocation and flight-clue panels. Return to Design to improve the aircraft.
7. Bottom notifications dismiss after five seconds. New notifications restart the timer, and each can be closed manually.

Drag handles over the aircraft to edit; drag empty space to orbit. Editing handles take priority over camera dragging. Design and free cameras retain wheel zoom, right-button pan and touch gestures without automatic recentering. Other inspectable flight cameras recenter after three seconds at 0.35/s. Results allow vertical dragging only. Collision correction first shortens the viewing distance, using upward displacement only when needed, with at most 8 m total displacement from the intended camera position. Removing the obstruction restores the original distance; correction never becomes a permanent orbit offset. The default chase view stays behind and above the aircraft without following roll; offset chase retains its diagonal view.

Every quality setting requests native browser antialiasing, subject to GPU support. Vertices use small terracotta circles, root chord 1–4 a large solid cyan circle, tip chord 2–3 a hollow purple circle, and mounting points blue diamonds. Root, tip and mounting handles have localized labels. Crowded vertices move outward with dashed guides to their actual positions. Valid layouts retain each handle's offset from its actual projected anchor rather than repeatedly resolving the 52 px spacing boundary. Guides and wing outlines still use actual geometry. Hit targets remain 44 px. Arrow-key editing, `Ctrl`, undo and numeric input remain available. Dragging an outward handle does not make the surface jump.

Physics runs at a fixed 60 Hz. Position, orientation and center of mass interpolate using the remaining accumulator time. Chase, side and pilot cameras use the same rendered pose, avoiding relative aircraft-camera jitter on high-refresh screens. Interpolation adds about one physics step of display latency, 16.67 ms, without changing collisions, scores or replays. Pause and page-visibility changes synchronize snapshots; termination shows the final physical state immediately. Catch-up stops after an 8 ms CPU budget per render frame; one due physics step always runs and cannot be interrupted. Excess whole ticks are discarded, keeping the fractional remainder. When physics cannot keep up, game time slows instead of increasing the physical timestep or replaying the backlog later.

Conventional aircraft use elevators and ailerons; Birdman uses pilot movement and the vertical tail. All challenges share the same physics rules. Debug force displays are available without an experimental mode.

Flight instruments show live angle of attack, actual lift-to-drag ratio, energy use, sink rate and distance–altitude data. Energy use is dissipated power divided by world-horizontal speed in J/m. During launch or at horizontal speed ≤0.05 m/s, it shows “—”; vertical airspeed does not count as horizontal travel. From release onward, the energy bars show potential energy, kinetic energy including rotation, and accumulated dissipation. Air drag, water drag and damping count as dissipation. Actual buoyancy work appears separately on the input side. External launch energy is excluded from the free-flight conservation baseline. The aircraft casts a shadow onto the lake at every quality level to help judge altitude.

Near the surface, induced drag decreases according to actual wing clearance and horizontally projected span; main-wing downwash on the horizontal tail also weakens. Dihedral and roll change this ground effect. Profile and separated-stall drag are not scaled; actual drag still dissipates energy. Design references remain in free air, without fixing performance to the original game's 42:1 or 50:1 ratios. See [formulas, sources and limits](docs/physics.md#水面地面效应).

The main wing and both tails are split every 0.8 m along the span, preserving the final partial segment. Each segment uses its deformed force point, normal, local airflow and clearance. Induced drag uses the aspect ratio of the connected wing section; downwash is area-weighted and does not multiply with segment count. The airfoil reference is the DAE-11-derived family used by Squalo, the 2017 Birdman gliding champion. The model uses multi-Reynolds-number XFOIL predictions for the original DAE-11, not TAKUMI-11 wind-tunnel measurements. Beam mass follows material density and actual equivalent sections. Skin and fuselage masses remain engineering allocations constrained by published empty weight, not measured component weights.

The main view continuously shows geometric performance, estimated stall speed, center of mass, static margin and aerodynamic data for all three surfaces. Main-wing and horizontal-tail incidence immediately change effective angle of attack, forces, moments and visible geometry. Stall speed uses sea-level total weight, main-wing area and the peak of the existing lift polar; it does not guarantee trim at the current incidence.

The main-wing root center can move from 0.5 m below to 1 m above the fuselage. Main wing offers a slider, exact input and 3D dragging. Dragging near zero snaps lightly to the fuselage; hold `Ctrl` to disable snapping. Zero height mounts directly. Nonzero height creates a central support plate as wide as the root chord. Its effective area density is approximately 0.833 kg/m², and it contributes to center of mass, inertia and stall speed. The main view shows its area and mass.

Center of mass uses a yellow-black sphere; center of lift uses a cyan-blue-black sphere and direction arrow; center of drag uses a red-black sphere and arrow. In Design, aircraft with engines also show an orange (#d58032) center-of-thrust marker at the mean of the equal-thrust engine mounts, with its arrow along body +X. Aircraft without engines hide it. Drag and thrust markers represent different forces. Markers have no 3D text. Their visual language references KSP; the drag marker is this project's extension. The GitHub icon beside the title opens the source repository.

The pilot lies prone. When not stalled, wingtip trails appear at all speeds, including launch. Opacity starts at zero at the tip, reaches one after 0.8 m, then fades over 18–60 m. Stall or flight termination clears trails immediately. The upper-left HUD updates every frame; charts and performance panels update independently at lower rates.

## Test environments

The **Test space** and **Wind tunnel** entries below the three challenges open the aircraft workshop first and preserve the current design. Neither creates leaderboard entries, input recordings, replay caches or flight-chart samples. Test space provides flight results and retries; wind tunnel stays open for continuous inspection until you return to design.

**Test space** is a 10 × 10 × 10 km volume, with distinct mountain ridges and valleys rising to 5000 m on the left, a 50 m central beach and ocean on the right. Four balloons suspend the 50 m platform, moved 2 km behind the centre to leave about 7 km ahead. Before starting, adjust its height from 0–5000 m, enable or disable the catapult and set its exit speed from 10–150 m/s. The aircraft follows height changes immediately. The catapult accelerates at least 1 G until reaching the target, coasts if needed and releases at the rail end. With the catapult disabled, actual engine thrust drives the same rail-takeoff solver as low-level flight. Settings lock once flight starts. Terrain, water, the volume boundary or manual stop end the flight. Results include actual takeoff height and speed, platform height, total path length and elapsed time (including the launch), maximum speed, G load and altitude, and fracture count.

Terrain and ocean reuse the scene's repeating surface textures and receive aircraft shadows. Sparse instanced trees populate green slopes. The zero-height deck uses a depth offset without moving its physical surface; the ocean uses a render-only waterline offset to prevent shoreline depth flicker. Shadow depth covers the ground receiver even when the platform is high.

**Wind tunnel** holds the aircraft in a central fixture, initially horizontal and facing right, with actual airflow at 0 m/s and a default target of 10 m/s. Six inward-facing dark grey concrete walls have a muted 1 m grid with 0.1 m ticks; walls cut away automatically when viewed from outside. Airflow travels right to left, with streak speed, length and density following the actual flow speed, disappearing at zero flow. Aero and structural data open by default. Range sliders and numeric inputs set flow speed (1–200 m/s), roll (±180°), pitch (±45°), aileron, elevator and rudder, all with one decimal place and 0.1-unit steps (control inputs use percent). **Turn on/off wind tunnel** ramps from the current speed to the configured target or zero; attitude and control inputs also transition smoothly while airflow is off. The selected side, top, front or free view is highlighted, including when cycling with C. The fixture balances net body loads while the existing aerodynamic and beam models compute local loads and fractures. Detached fragments move in the airflow and settle at the room boundary. Breakup never ends the session. **Restore aircraft** repairs the structure and removes debris while preserving airflow, attitude, settings, view and elapsed time. Use **Return to design** to leave. This is a steady-flow inspection tool using the game's aerodynamic model, without wall interference or fluid simulation.

Wind-tunnel roll and pitch retain their travel-speed limits of 45°/s and 30°/s, easing through two smoothing stages. Angular velocity and acceleration remain continuous when starting, stopping or changing the target. Smoothing is checked at **10 m/s**, including both axes, reversals and inverted attitudes. The fixture still applies actual loads to the structure. Restoring the aircraft preserves an ongoing attitude transition. The bottom-centre wind-speed display follows actual airflow, including when airflow is off. Free-camera collision correction keeps the observation target fixed and restores the requested distance after clearance returns.

Run `npx playwright test tests/issue-40.spec.ts` to verify both environments on desktop and portrait/landscape mobile viewports. `tests/laboratory.test.ts` covers terrain, catapult acceleration/coasting, powered rail takeoff, boundaries, smooth relative-flow controls, bounded attitude acceleration, breakup/restoration and recording exclusion. `tests/scene.test.ts` also covers camera clearance without target drift.

## Sound

The top speaker icon opens sound settings: master mute, music volume and effects volume. Preferences stay in the current browser. Audio starts after the first click or keypress. Browser autoplay restrictions do not block play. Pausing or moving the page to the background freezes sound and music position; resuming continues playback. Returning to Design restores background music without repeating launch events.

The BGM is Erik Satie's *Gymnopedie No. 1*, recorded by Kevin MacLeod: an approximately 3-minute-7-second soft piano recording, looped with a fade-in. Music volume drops further during flight. The recording uses CC BY 4.0. Attribution, official download source and transcoding notes are in [`public/audio/LICENSE.txt`](public/audio/LICENSE.txt), also linked in sound settings. Music ships with the site and does not depend on external hotlinks.

UI clicks, success and error notifications, two-layer wind, steam release and mechanical separation are synthesized with native Web Audio, without an audio dependency or third-party effects samples. Wind loudness and filter frequency follow actual speed smoothly. Steam starts only when a catapult begins accelerating. The transition from launch to release triggers one mechanical latch, low-frequency impact, metallic resonance and short steam burst. Low-level rail launches play neither steam nor catapult separation. The separation sound references the distinct impact of rocket staging without using KSP audio assets. Pause, replay and retry do not depend on wall-clock timers. Catapult physics retains the two-second constant-acceleration model, without steam-fluid or pressure simulation.

Crossing the starting gate, each checkpoint and the finish triggers an approximately 0.24-second two-layer tone. It remains audible without overpowering ambient sound; the finish tone has a slightly higher pitch. Actual timing state and checkpoint progress trigger playback. Frame synchronization and pause/resume do not repeat it, and results do not cut off the finish sound. Mute, effects volume and background freezing use the shared audio channels; resuming does not replay missed tones.

Jet audio uses dedicated oscillators and filtered noise. Pitch, loudness and spectrum follow physical engine speed rather than raw throttle. Shutdown fades smoothly. It uses the same mute, effects-volume, pause and background-freeze behavior, with no new dependency or third-party effects.

`tests/audio.spec.ts` measures native browser audio nodes for wind and engines, covering speed, engine speed, mute, pause and resume. AudioWorklet captures actual separation and gate audio to measure band energy, loudness, transients and decay. Coverage includes start and finish, repeated state, mute, zero volume, pause, background, replay and the absence of catapult audio in low-level flight. Run `npx playwright test tests/audio.spec.ts --project=desktop-chromium`. Audio-load failures leave flight available and appear in sound settings.

## Saving and replay

Design provides one Save action. A new name creates a user aircraft; overwriting an existing user name requires confirmation. System aircraft are always listed in the hangar and cannot be overwritten, renamed or deleted; copy or export them to make an editable saved variant. Saving an edited system aircraft creates a user record. Incompatible aircraft are greyed out and cannot be loaded. User aircraft retain load, rename, copy, delete and JSON export actions. Successful loading closes the hangar; damaged records preserve the design and keep the hangar open. Deletion requires confirmation. Storage failures show a notification without replacing valid records. Clearing site data deletes user aircraft, not system presets; export important designs as backups.
Aircraft JSON import saves the original validated design to the hangar and closes it. Compatible designs load immediately; incompatible designs remain saved without replacing the current aircraft. Malformed files and storage failures preserve the design and leave the hangar open.

Birdman distance is measured horizontally from the platform front to the aircraft's rearmost body point, not its center of mass or accumulated path length. Lateral offsets use a radial measurement convention. New records remain separate from the old center-of-mass-distance records; old data is neither deleted nor mixed into the new ranking.

Undo and redo retain up to 60 snapshots. `Ctrl/Cmd+Z` undoes; `Ctrl/Cmd+Shift+Z` redoes. A parameter drag creates a snapshot when it ends. Saving after undo or redo follows the same name-confirmation rule. The hangar contains exactly four system aircraft, with localized names: Birdman basic, high-performance glider, basic twin-engine and basic single-engine. Powered aircraft show cruise references: twin-engine 72% throttle at 30 m/s; single-engine 55% throttle at 17 m/s. These are reference values, not automatic throttle settings.

Results can replay the latest flight or export it as JSON. Import replay is available on the home screen and in the workshop. Files contain the challenge, complete aircraft, launch disturbance and every recorded control frame. Fixed-rate overload accumulation and progressive fracture use physics version `fixed60-accumulated-fracture-v16`, isolating earlier replays and rankings. Slight continuous overload breaks a joint within five physics ticks; extreme overload can break it in one. Unloading below the strength limit clears the accumulation; this is a game failure-delay rule, not material fatigue. A fin-mounted horizontal tail follows its supporting fin during deformation and fracture, including subsequent independent breaks. Long-wing contact queries use per-body and support-branch indices; reproducible measurements are documented in [contact performance](docs/contact-performance.md). Imports require the same physics version and a SHA-256 match of the deterministic map geometry and rules. Invalid files leave the current design intact. Imported replays do not update personal records; truncated recordings pause at their last frame. Files are limited to 16 MiB and 36,000 frames.

Keyframes use native gzip compression inside the JSON file. Decompressed keyframe data is limited to 128 MiB. Nearby full-state caching covers up to 0.5 s on either side, capped at 121 states and a conservative 64 MiB retained-state estimate. Input-only files from the current physics version build fixed keyframes once before seeking; newly exported files include them.

Pilot view uses a common open cockpit instead of the aircraft's exterior model. The HUD projects a 1.2 × 0.8 m plane fixed to the airframe into native SVG paths and text, avoiding CSS 3D bitmap scaling and shadows. It is 20% smaller than the previous HUD. Looking around moves it out of the center. Small, bounded head translations respond to body loads and freeze with simulation time; reduced-motion preferences disable them. Returning to other cameras restores the exterior.

Adaptive control authority tightens immediately for safety and recovers with a 0.75 s time constant. Control surfaces move at 1.25 normalized units per second. This damps repeated limit recovery under sustained full stick; real neutral-load overloads can still break the aircraft. The physics version is updated to keep old scores separate.

 Birdman energy-cost charts scale to normal airborne samples using an outlier fence. Water-contact spikes and other values above the scale retain their raw samples and appear as triangles at the top, with exact values on hover. Result charts retain at least 720 px width and 235 px height; narrow screens scroll the chart horizontally rather than shrinking labels, and the result panel scrolls vertically when needed. Right axes use compact 50-unit spacing. Course guidance is lavender, separate from amber checkpoint gates. Wingtip trails are thinner, use at most 95% material opacity and fade over longer portions of their length.
 
Live flight offers chase, locked, rear-quarter and pilot cameras; C cycles through those four. Replay offers all eleven views, including free orbit and orthographic inspection. Locked follows with an 83 ms response time and allows small yaw/pitch drags (±0.18/±0.12 radians). Its camera-aligned HUD shifts slightly with directional G loads, including ground support and impacts. Pilot mouse dragging down looks down. Reduced motion disables camera lag and load sway. Free replay view follows aircraft translation and retains user orbit and wheel zoom without automatic recentering. R restarts only during pause or on results. The home replay-import entry uses the same secondary-button style and size as challenge entries.

## Checks

```sh
npm run check
npm test
npm run test:e2e
```

Physics regressions set the geometry, material strength and engine count needed by each scenario explicitly instead of assuming that a system preset stays unchanged. Preset validation remains covered separately. Unit tests retain physical boundaries, energy accounting, fractures, deterministic replay and cache limits; duplicate replay runs and assertions that only pin visual styling or translated wording are omitted.

Complete-flight and rail-launch regressions use longer per-test timeouts to accommodate structural and contact integration on shared CI CPUs. Expensive setup yields between integration batches so the test worker can respond to the runner. Vitest's default five-second timeout is not a performance metric. Use `npx vitest run --maxWorkers=1` when sharing CPU with browser rendering.

Browser tests start the development server by default. To use an existing server when CI is enabled:

```sh
PLAYWRIGHT_NO_SERVER=1 npm run test:e2e
```

Install Playwright Chromium with `npx playwright install chromium`. Interaction tests cover component editing, flight, controls, water termination, scores, further editing, hangar and replay.

## Performance

```sh
# Start npm run dev first
npm run perf
```

Results go to `docs/performance-results.json`. The tool samples browser rAF intervals independently rather than treating target FPS as measured performance. Configure durations with `PERF_SAMPLE_MS`, `PERF_LONG_MS` and `PERF_RESET_MS`. `CDP_URL` connects to a real device browser; see the [mobile validation guide](docs/mobile-performance.md).

Select Performance to open the normally hidden panel. It shows mean/P95/P99 frame times, physics time, CPU render-submission time, geometry-update time, draw calls, triangles and estimated geometry/texture memory. Submission time is not GPU execution time.

**60 FPS on ordinary phones still requires real-device validation. Desktop and emulated phone viewports do not establish this target.** High/Medium/Low quality and automatic downshifting reduce pixel ratio, shadows and environment detail. Sampling and hysteresis prevent frequent switching.

Aircraft materials, pilot geometry and body meshes are reused. Continuous edits coalesce within each render frame, rebuilding only changed surfaces. Dragging does not repeatedly resize the canvas or traverse the whole scene to estimate memory. The aerodynamic core reuses math objects; flight inputs use preallocated TypedArrays. Auto starts conservatively at Low, then adapts to stable frame times.

## Code map

- `src/model.ts`: standalone aircraft definitions, bounds, quadrilateral geometry and presets.
- `src/materials.ts`: stable material catalog, default beam sections and closed-tube section formulas.
- `src/structure.ts`: segmented quasi-static beams, stress, fracture ownership and full inertia tensors.
- `src/physics.ts`: segmented aerodynamics, body and fragment integration, mass/inertia, contact, scoring and input replay.
- `src/scene.ts`: smoothly deformed thick airfoils, fracture faces and fragments, environment, cameras, resource cleanup, quality and performance monitoring.
- `src/App.svelte`: page state, fixed-60-Hz accumulator, controls and UI throttling.
- `src/WingEditor.svelte`, `src/Parameter.svelte`: exact coordinate and parameter editing. The scene layer handles 3D dragging.
- `src/storage.ts`: local hangar, import boundaries, grouped records and history snapshots.
- `tests/`: geometry, storage, physics and Playwright interaction checks.

## Model and scope

The aerodynamic model is simplified for a game. Design parameters change mass, inertia, lift, drag and moments. It has no CFD or vortex solver, random scores or fixed flight paths; it is not an engineering simulator.

- Original-game research: [docs/original-game-analysis.md](docs/original-game-analysis.md). Observed / Inferred / Unknown distinguish evidence from interpretation.
- Physics, coordinates, coefficients and limits: [docs/physics.md](docs/physics.md).
- Performance sampling and real-device reproduction: [docs/mobile-performance.md](docs/mobile-performance.md).
- Historical preset and trim measurements: [docs/flight-balance.md](docs/flight-balance.md).
- Real-device connection evidence: [docs/device-validation.md](docs/device-validation.md).
- Bottlenecks, before/after optimization and measured results: [docs/performance-analysis.md](docs/performance-analysis.md).

There are no accounts, servers, online leaderboards or complex water simulation. Structural failure occurs at aerodynamic segment boundaries, without arbitrary cracks or skin tearing. Main wing and tails use fixed lightweight airfoil representations; airfoil sections are not editable. Four-point editing keeps root and tip pairs at equal spanwise coordinates to prevent self-intersection. Mounts remain supported by the fuselage, but valid geometry does not guarantee aerodynamic stability. Test and trim the aircraft in flight.
